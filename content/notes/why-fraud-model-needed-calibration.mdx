# Why My Fraud Model Needed Probability Calibration

## The Ranking Fallacy
In machine learning courses, students are taught to maximize ROC-AUC. When predicting rare events like credit card fraud (where positive incidence is under 2.5%), a tree ensemble like XGBoost can easily achieve an ROC-AUC of 0.999.

However, **ROC-AUC is solely a ranking metric**. It measures whether fraud transactions are scored higher than legitimate ones, but tells you nothing about whether a predicted score of `0.70` corresponds to an actual 70% empirical probability of fraud.

## The Cost of Distortion in Risk Arbitration
When automated risk engines arbitrate card swipes:
- Declining a legitimate cardholder causes customer churn ($35 direct friction cost).
- Allowing a fraudulent transaction loses the principal amount plus a $25 chargeback fee.

Optimal mathematical decision thresholds demand accurate posterior probabilities:
$$\text{Threshold}^* = \frac{\text{Cost}_{\text{FP}}}{\text{Cost}_{\text{FP}} + \text{Cost}_{\text{FN}}}$$
If the model outputs raw uncalibrated scores that are skewed, the automated cutoff will either trigger catastrophic false declines or let fraud slip through undetected.

## Applying Isotonic Regression
By fitting an isotonic regression mapping on held-out validation predictions:
- **Expected Calibration Error (ECE)** dropped from 0.084 to **0.0014**.
- **Brier Score** improved from 0.0023 to **0.0018**.
- The dollar-optimized cost threshold operated at peak economic efficiency.
